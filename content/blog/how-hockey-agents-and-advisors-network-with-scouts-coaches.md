Search "hockey scouting relationships" and almost everything that comes back is written for a 15-year-old trying to get noticed — how to get seen, how to build a highlight reel, how to catch a scout's eye at a showcase. None of it is written for the people on the other side of that transaction: the agents and advisors who have to build and maintain the actual relationships that make a career possible.

That gap matters because the job looks nothing like its public image, and it isn't one job — it's two related ones. Agents are NHLPA-certified, work on commission, and represent players once they're off the NCAA-eligible track and into the draft or pro process. Advisors work with NCAA-track and junior players specifically, for a flat, pre-agreed fee, precisely because NCAA amateurism rules bar those players from having a commission-based agent at all. Neither certification nor commission is part of what an advisor does — that's not a gap in the role, it's the design of it. Different compensation structure, different eligible client, same underlying work: building relationships with the people around a player long before any deal exists.

theScore's reporting on the profession [put it plainly](https://www.thescore.com/nhl/news/2377141/part-1-being-an-agent-is-far-more-complicated-than-negotiating-contracts), quoting an NCAA Division I coach who described what agents actually do day to day: "You're a babysitter, you're a butler, you're a concierge, you're a ticket finagler, you're an organizer of events…" The same description holds for advisors working a junior or prep roster, often earlier and with less margin for error, since an advisor's entire client base is still years from a paycheck. Contract negotiation, where it applies, is a fraction of either job. The rest is relationship management — with the player, the family, and everyone around the rink who has a hand in that player's development. Most of those relationships exist for years before any fee is ever discussed. This is a guide to building them, whichever side of the agent/advisor line you work.

## Who's Actually in the Network

People talk about "the network" like it's one thing, but it's really five overlapping circles, each serving a different function. Understanding what each relationship is actually for — not just that it's valuable — changes how you invest in it. Most of these apply equally whether you're a certified agent or a flat-fee advisor; a couple skew toward one side or the other, and it's worth being clear about which.

### Area and team scouts

Scouts are usually the earliest source of intel on a player's trajectory — who's trending up, who's dealing with an injury nobody's announced, which 16-year-old just had a growth spurt that changes his projection. They see players across more games than almost anyone else in the ecosystem. This matters just as much to an advisor tracking a 15-year-old's development as it does to an agent tracking a 19-year-old's draft stock. A scout who trusts you will tell you things before they're public. One who doesn't will give you nothing beyond what's already on a stat sheet.

### Junior and college coaches

Coaches see a player every day — practice habits, how he responds to being benched, whether he's coachable when things go badly. That's a completely different read than what you get from box scores. Coaches are also the gatekeepers on ice time, and ice time is the thing that actually builds or kills draft stock. This is, if anything, more central to an advisor's work than an agent's — advisors are operating inside exactly the junior and college pipeline these coaches run, often for the full multi-year stretch before a player is even draft-eligible. A coach who trusts you will give you an honest read on where a player really stands, not a diplomatic one.

### GMs and front-office staff

Front-office relationships matter most at the pro and near-pro level — trade context, waiver situations, organizational depth-chart realities that never make the press. This is largely agent territory, since it applies once a client has turned pro or is on the direct edge of it; advisors' clients typically haven't reached that stage yet. A GM won't tip you off to a move, but a GM who trusts you will help you understand why a client's role is changing before it blindsides him.

### Other agents and advisors

People in this business tend to think of each other only as competitors, which is a mistake — and it's especially a mistake across the agent/advisor line, where the relationship is often complementary rather than competitive. An advisor who has worked with a player for three years through juniors is frequently the person best positioned to hand that player off to an agent once he's ready to turn pro — and a good handoff, done with the family's full knowledge, is worth more to everyone's reputation than either side trying to hold on past the point where the arrangement still makes sense. Beyond that handoff relationship, the agents and advisors who last in this business build genuine reciprocal relationships with a handful of colleagues — referring a player you can't properly serve, splitting intel on a shared market, vouching for each other with a coach or family. Reciprocity here compounds the same way it does with scouts and coaches, and it's underused.

### Billet families and support staff

Billet parents, equipment managers, athletic trainers, skills coaches — they see a player's daily reality in a way no one else does. Sleep habits, how he's handling homesickness, whether an "upper body injury" is actually something serious. These relationships rarely make it into how people talk about networking, but for advisors especially — whose clients are often living away from home for the first time in juniors or prep hockey — they're often the most honest source of information you'll get.

## How Trust Actually Gets Built (From the Coach's Side of the Glass)

Most advice on this topic is written from the agent's or advisor's point of view — how to get in front of a coach, how to make an ask. Flip it around, and the picture looks different.

The Junior Hockey News runs a recurring series called ["Confessions of a Junior Hockey Coach,"](https://thejuniorhockeynews.com/confessions-of-a-junior-hockey-coach-how-scouting-works/) written from inside a junior program, and the throughline is consistent: coaches trust what they see with their own eyes over what anyone tells them. "The most important part of being scouted is being seen in person," the coach writes. "There is not a substitute." That standard doesn't stop at players — it applies to the agents and advisors around them too. Someone who shows up to games, in person, over a season, reads completely differently than someone who only calls or texts when they want something.

The companion piece, ["Adviser or Agent,"](https://thejuniorhockeynews.com/confessions-of-a-junior-hockey-coach-adviser-or-agent/) gets more pointed about what erodes that trust — and it's a useful reminder that coaches are watching conduct, not job titles. The author's concern is representatives, whichever side of the agent/advisor line they're on, who service their hyped prospects intensively while letting the rest of a roster go cold — showing up for the kid who's a lock for the second round or a top NCAA commitment and disappearing on the depth players who might need more help, not less. Coaches notice. A coach who watches someone play favorites within their own client list draws an obvious conclusion about how that person will treat every future player they don't rate as highly. Consistency across a full roster isn't just good practice — it's the signal coaches actually read to decide whether an agent or advisor is worth trusting with the next kid who comes through the program.

## Where These Relationships Actually Happen

None of this gets built over email. It happens in specific physical places, repeated over years, and the type of place changes what kind of relationship you're building.

### Rinks, showcases, and tournaments

This is the baseline — the rink is where sustained, unglamorous presence happens, for agents and advisors alike. Not one showcase, but the same tournaments and the same rinks, season after season, until a coach or scout stops treating you as a stranger and starts treating you as a known quantity. There's no shortcut here. Showing up once doesn't register. Showing up for five seasons does.

### Development camps and combines

Camps and combines compress a lot of relationship-building into a short window — multiple teams' staff, scouts, and other agents and advisors all in one building for a few days. They're higher-density opportunities, but they only work as a supplement to the year-round presence at rinks, not a replacement for it. Someone you meet for the first time at a combine has no track record with you yet.

### Industry conferences and league meetings

This is where agent-to-agent and agent-to-league relationships mostly form — NHLPA-adjacent meetings and industry conferences where you're talking to certified peers and league staff rather than players and families. It's a different register of networking, more professional-association than rinkside, and it's specific to the certified-agent side of the business, since it runs through NHLPA structures advisors aren't part of. Advisors build the equivalent professional relationships elsewhere in the junior and college hockey world — league and conference meetings, prep and junior program networks, and their own peer relationships with other advisors — which is where the referral and reputational infrastructure of that side of the business actually lives.

## What to Offer Before You Ask for Anything

The agents and advisors with durable networks operate on a simple rule: give before you take. Reciprocity is the actual mechanism that makes a network last, not a nice sentiment layered on top of it.

In practice that means sharing accurate intel with scouts and coaches, not just extracting it — flagging something you noticed about a player's conditioning, correcting a rumor you know is wrong, being straight about a client's situation even when the honest answer isn't flattering. It means referring a player to a colleague when you know you can't properly service him yourself — or, for an advisor, making the handoff to an agent at the right time rather than trying to hang on past it — rather than signing everyone who'll say yes and letting the marginal ones go neglected, which is exactly the behavior that erodes coach trust, as noted above. And it means being a resource a coach or scout can call for a straight answer even on a question that does nothing for you — no angle, no client attached, just a reliable read because you've been in the building long enough to have one.

None of that pays off on any predictable schedule. It pays off over years, unevenly, in referrals you can't trace back to a specific conversation. That's the trade.

## Keeping the Network Warm Between Signings

The relationship-building that gets talked about is the visible kind — the rink, the camp, the conference. The relationship-building that actually separates the agents and advisors who last from the ones who don't is the unsexy maintenance in between: a check-in call in the middle of the off-season when there's nothing to ask for, a follow-up with a coach after a tournament regardless of how a client performed, staying in touch with a coach whose player signed somewhere else — or, for an advisor, whose player moved on to an agent — instead of disappearing the moment that player is off your board.

That last one trips up a lot of people. Once a player signs, or doesn't sign with you, or ages out of your lane, the instinct is to move on. But that coach is still in the building next season, still talking to other coaches, still forming an opinion of you based on whether you kept showing up. Some agents and advisors track these touchpoints in some kind of system just so nobody falls through the cracks over a long season — nothing more complicated than a way to remember who you haven't talked to in a while. The mechanism matters less than actually doing it.

## The Line Between Networking and Bird-Dogging

There's a version of "networking" that isn't networking at all — it's recruiting, and it's a real problem in this sport. theScore's reporting cites 190 agents certified by the NHLPA alongside [at least another 200 uncertified "bird dog" recruiters](https://www.thescore.com/nhl/news/2377141/part-1-being-an-agent-is-far-more-complicated-than-negotiating-contracts) — people building relationships with players and families well before those players are teenagers, with no defined role, no fee agreement, and no accountability attached. That reporting also notes pitches for representation landing on families as early as 12 or 13, years before any of it is appropriate or useful to the player.

It's worth being precise about who this actually describes, because it is not advisors. A bird dog operates entirely outside any professional framework: no certification, no disclosed fee, no defined relationship with the family beyond an informal one built on proximity to a very young player. An advisor is the opposite of that — a legitimate, disclosed, flat-fee professional relationship with a player already on the NCAA or junior track, doing exactly the kind of work the NCAA's amateurism rules anticipate by keeping commission-based agents out of that stage of a player's career. Advisors aren't NHLPA-certified, but that's not a gap in accountability — certification isn't part of the role to begin with. An advisor's accountability runs through reputation, a disclosed fee arrangement, and a name the family and the program both know, not through a union credential. Lumping that in with bird-dogging gets the actual problem backwards: the concern isn't that advisors operate without NHLPA certification, it's that bird dogs operate without any accountability structure at all.

This distinction matters because bird-dogging does real damage to everyone else in the ecosystem, agents and advisors alike. Every uncertified recruiter cold-calling a 12-year-old's parents makes every legitimate agent's and advisor's job harder, because it teaches coaches and families to be suspicious of early contact generally — including the legitimate kind an advisor is supposed to provide. The NHLPA has discussed — though not implemented — a rule restricting certified agents from contacting players under 16. Sweden's players' association, SICO, already has one: [TSN reported](https://www.tsn.ca/chl/nhlpa-considering-restricting-agents-from-contacting-players-under-16-1.705352) that Sweden has barred agent contact before a player's 16-year-old season for roughly 15 years, with SICO's president asking the obvious question — "What good comes of having agents sign up as advisors to kids who are 10 or 11 years old?" There's no equivalent rule for agents in North America yet, which is precisely why staying well inside ethical lines — for agents and advisors both — is a choice you have to make voluntarily rather than one the rulebook makes for you. ## Common Ways Agents and Advisors Burn Bridges

Most damage to a network isn't dramatic — it's a handful of repeatable mistakes that coaches and scouts remember for years, and they cut across agents and advisors equally.

Treating players unequally once they're signed is the big one, and it's the same failure mode described earlier from the coach's side of the glass: full attention for the projected first-rounder or top recruit, radio silence for everyone else on your list. It shows up fast in how you [manage a roster](/blog/how-hockey-agents-manage-their-roster), and coaches notice which of your clients you actually show up for.

Badmouthing competitors to a coach is another. Coaches talk to each other, and to other agents and advisors, constantly — a jab aimed at a competitor almost always gets back to that competitor, and it tells the coach more about your judgment than about the person you criticized.

Misrepresenting your role is a third, and it's not just a trust problem — it's a compliance one. Presenting yourself as an advisor while functioning like an agent (or the reverse) can violate NCAA and major-junior eligibility rules that hinge on exactly that distinction: flat fee versus commission, certified versus not, pre- versus post-NCAA-track. Our [CHL/NCAA eligibility guide](/blog/chl-ncaa-eligibility-rules-2025) breaks down where that line sits and what crossing it costs a player.

And then there's the simplest one: over-promising and under-communicating. Telling a family what they want to hear about a projection, then going quiet when reality doesn't match it. Coaches and scouts remember whose word was good — agent or advisor, it's the same reputation ledger.

## Playing the Long Game

None of this pays off on a draft-cycle timeline. The agents and advisors with real staying power in this business didn't build their client pipeline from cold outreach — most of it traces back to someone who vouched for them years earlier: a coach who remembered how they treated a kid who never made it, a scout who'd seen them show up at the same rink for five straight seasons, another agent or advisor who sent a referral because the relationship was genuinely reciprocal. That's not a growth hack. It's just what happens when you treat every relationship as one you're still going to need in ten years, because you probably are.

## FAQ

**How do hockey agents and advisors actually find out about players before they're well known?**
Mostly through the informal network described above — scouts, coaches, billet families, and other agents and advisors — rather than any database or public ranking. By the time a player shows up on a public list, people who are plugged into the right rinks and coaching staffs have usually known about him for a season or more.

**What's the actual difference between a hockey "advisor" and an "agent," and does it matter for networking?**
Yes — it changes who you're eligible to work with and how you're compensated. Advisors work with NCAA-track and junior players under flat, upfront fees, aren't NHLPA-certified because certification isn't part of the role, and can't be tied to a player's future earnings without jeopardizing his college eligibility. Agents are NHLPA-certified, work on commission, and represent players once they're off the NCAA-eligible track. Misrepresenting which one you are to a coach or family is both a trust problem and a compliance one — see our [CHL/NCAA eligibility guide](/blog/chl-ncaa-eligibility-rules-2025) for the specifics.

**Is a bird dog the same thing as an advisor?**
No, and conflating them is a common mistake. An advisor is a legitimate, disclosed, flat-fee professional relationship with a player already on the NCAA or junior track — accountable through reputation and a documented fee arrangement, even without NHLPA certification, because certification was never part of the role. A bird dog is an informal, uncertified recruiter with no fee agreement and no defined relationship at all, typically targeting players well before their teens. The accountability gap is the entire distinction.

**Are hockey agents allowed to contact players and their families directly, and at what age?**
There's currently no formal NHLPA rule setting a minimum age for certified agents, though the union has discussed restricting contact with players under 16. Sweden's players' association already enforces that restriction for agents. Advisors operate on a different track — their clients are, by design, already in their teens and on the NCAA or junior pipeline — but the same principle applies to both roles: staying well clear of contact with very young players who have no real need for representation yet is a voluntary standard worth holding regardless of what the rulebook requires. Our [certification guide](/blog/nhlpa-agent-certification-guide) covers the current rules in more detail.

**How do you build scout and coach relationships if you weren't a former pro player?**
Playing background helps open a first conversation, but it doesn't sustain a relationship by itself, whether you're an agent or an advisor. Consistency does — showing up at the same rinks over multiple seasons, giving accurate information, and treating every player on your list the same way. Coaches remember reliability far longer than they remember a résumé.

**Do junior and college coaches actually trust agents and advisors?**
Unevenly, and for good reason — bird-dog recruiting of very young players, and representatives who only service their top prospects, have made plenty of coaches skeptical by default. That skepticism is aimed at the accountability problem bird dogs represent, not at legitimate advisors doing disclosed, flat-fee work within the rules. Consistent, in-person, evenly-applied conduct is exactly how an individual agent or advisor separates themselves from the reputation the more careless corners of the profession have earned.

**How long does it realistically take to build a usable referral network as a new agent or advisor?**
Multiple seasons, not one draft cycle. People who expect a showcase or two to produce a working network are almost always disappointed — coaches and scouts need to see you show up repeatedly, over years, before they treat you as someone worth vouching for.
