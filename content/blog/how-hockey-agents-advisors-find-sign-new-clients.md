Every agent or advisor gets asked some version of the same question at a dinner party: "So how do you actually find players?" The honest answer is unsatisfying to non-hockey people — there's no database, no cold-calling script, no magic algorithm. It's a small industry built on relationships that take years to develop and can evaporate over one bad phone call. If you've been doing this for a while, none of that is news. What's worth revisiting is how the mechanics of finding and signing clients have shifted as the recruiting age has crept younger, the population of uncertified "bird dog" recruiters has grown, and families have gotten savvier about vetting the person they're trusting with their kid's career — and about knowing which of those people is even the right one to be talking to at a given stage.

## Agent, Advisor, or Bird Dog? Why the Distinction Matters

Hockey representation gets talked about as if "agent" is the only word that matters, and that's a mistake worth fixing before anything else. Three different roles operate in this space, and they're not interchangeable:

An **agent** is NHLPA-certified, works on commission, and represents players once they're off the NCAA-eligible track — entering the draft or pro process, where NHLPA regulations govern contact, conduct, and fees. An **advisor** is not NHLPA-certified, because certification isn't required or even relevant to the role — advisors work for a flat, pre-agreed fee, specifically with NCAA-track and junior players, because NCAA amateurism rules bar those players from having a commission-based agent in the first place. That's not a lesser or shadier version of "agent" — it's a distinct, necessary professional category that exists because the rules of college hockey require it. A **bird dog**, by contrast, is an informal, uncertified recruiter — sometimes overlapping with a scout's role — who builds early relationships with very young players outside any professional framework at all, answerable to no one.

Most of what follows in this guide applies to agents and advisors alike — finding prospects, building trust with families, differentiating your service, playing the long game. Where the two roles diverge, we'll say so explicitly. And where bird dogs are the actual problem, we won't blur that into a knock on legitimate advisors, who are already operating with less formal accountability than certified agents and don't deserve to be lumped in with recruiters who have none at all.

## The Market Is Smaller and Faster-Moving Than People Think

Start with scale, because it explains almost everything else about how this business works. There are roughly 190 agents certified through the [NHLPA's agent program](https://www.nhlpa.com/certified-agents/). Alongside them is a real, if uncounted, population of advisors doing legitimate flat-fee work with NCAA-track and junior players — work that exists precisely because those players can't have a commission agent. And then, separately, according to [theScore's reporting](https://www.thescore.com/nhl/news/2377141), there are at least 200 uncertified bird-dog recruiters working rinks, tournaments, and showcases without ever being accountable to any conduct rules at all. Three populations, three very different accountability profiles, all competing for a talent pool that everyone can see at the same events.

You cannot out-advertise your way into this market — there's no billboard that makes a 15-year-old's parents trust you. But because it's so small, reputation compounds fast in both directions. A junior coach who had a good experience with you three years ago will mention your name unprompted. A family that felt pressured by you will mention that too, and it travels just as far. The real question behind "how do I find clients" isn't really about discovery — it's about being the person a scout thinks to call, and the agent or advisor a family picks when five others have already reached out. Both take longer to build than most new entrants to the business expect, and both can be undone quickly.

## Where Prospects Actually Get Identified

### Rinks, tournaments, and showcases

The obvious answer is still mostly right: agents and advisors alike identify prospects at youth and junior rinks, regional and national tournaments, and league-affiliated showcases. These events are where the on-ice evaluation happens, but they're also where the relationship-building happens — with other agents and advisors, with billet families, with tournament organizers who'll remember you next season.

### Leaning on scouts, coaches, and GMs — and what you owe them in return

Very few agents or advisors discover a player cold. Most hear about a name from someone already inside the pipeline. The mechanic isn't unique to hockey — a similar breakdown from [National Football Post's look at how agents find clients](https://www.nationalfootballpost.com/how-do-agents-find-new-clients/) describes experienced agents having "direct access to area scouts, college directors and even GMs who willingly share reports," and the same dynamic plays out at rinks across Canada, the U.S., and Europe, whether the person building the relationship is a certified agent working the draft-eligible end of the pipeline or an advisor working with a 14-year-old years out from any of that. Scouts and coaches aren't handing out tips for free. They pass along intel because the relationship runs both ways — someone who helps a scout make a connection, shares information about a prospect's situation, or simply treats them like a peer instead of a source gets remembered the next time something worth knowing comes up. Agents and advisors who show up only when they need something get frozen out fast; the ones who stay useful in the gaps between recruiting seasons are the ones still getting calls five years later.

That relationship only pays off if you know what to do with the tip. Vetting matters as much as access — checking a prospect's character and off-ice conduct, not just their measurables, before you invest real time in a family. A kid with great tape and a bad locker-room reputation is a problem you're inheriting, not a client you're signing.

### Reading a player's on-ice fit, not just their stat line

Points and plus-minus tell you what already happened. Projecting fit — role, pace, how a player's skill set translates to the next level of competition — is the harder skill, and it's the one that actually determines whether you're representing someone with a realistic path forward. This matters even more once a player is draft-eligible, when the evaluation window compresses and the stakes go up; our [draft day prep checklist for advisors](/blog/draft-day-prep-checklist-hockey-advisors) covers how to get a prospect's file in order before that window closes — work that typically falls to the advisor who's been with the player through the junior and NCAA-track years, often in close coordination with the agent who takes over once the player turns pro.

## Agents, Advisors, and the Ethics of Recruiting a Minor

This is where hockey representation looks genuinely different from most other sports, and it's worth being candid about it rather than glossing over it — starting with getting the terminology right, because a lot of the public confusion here comes from calling something an "agent" problem when it's really an advisor-and-bird-dog problem.

### Who's actually doing the recruiting at each age

The players at the center of most of this debate — 12- to 16-year-olds — are, almost by definition, NCAA- or junior-track prospects who are years away from being draft-eligible and legally can't be signed to a commission agent under NCAA amateurism rules. The person building a relationship with that family is an advisor, not a certified agent, even when the family or the press calls it "signing with an agent." A certified agent isn't typically the one courting a 13-year-old, because there's no commission relationship to offer that player yet. Keeping that straight matters, because it changes what oversight actually applies.

### What's actually regulated (and what isn't)

Certified agents operate under the NHLPA's [regulations governing agent certification](/blog/nhlpa-agent-certification-guide), which cover conduct, disclosure, and fee limits for anyone representing a player in dealings with an NHL club. That's real oversight — but it only applies to certified agents, and by design it was never meant to reach advisors, since advisors work with a population of players the NHLPA's certification framework doesn't govern. That's not a loophole so much as a structural fact of how NCAA amateurism rules and NHLPA jurisdiction fit together. Legitimate advisors stay accountable through different means — a written, disclosed flat-fee arrangement instead of a hidden commission, a track record and references a family can actually check, and a reputation that, in a market this small, travels just as fast as a certified agent's does. The real gap isn't advisors operating without NHLPA certification — it's bird dogs operating without any of that: no disclosed fee structure, no verifiable track record, and no professional framework holding them to anything.

### The under-16 contact debate and where it stands

The NHLPA has discussed — but not implemented — a rule barring certified agents from contacting players under 16 or their families. Per [TSN's reporting](https://www.tsn.ca/chl/nhlpa-considering-restricting-agents-from-contacting-players-under-16-1.705352), the union has had the idea under internal discussion for roughly a year, with NHLPA spokesman Jonathan Weatherdon confirming a decision "isn't imminent," largely because policing it across an international pipeline is genuinely hard. Sweden's hockey federation already runs something close to this model, with certification suspensions and fines up to 50,000 SEK for agents who violate it — a system Frölunda GM Christian Lechtaler has praised, asking pointedly, "What good comes of having agents sign up as advisors to kids who are 10 or 11 years old?" Lechtaler's own phrasing is a good illustration of the confusion this guide is trying to clear up — even people inside the game reach for "agent" as the default word when what they're actually describing is uncertified, unaccountable recruiting of children. TSN's sourcing also found agencies quietly using unofficial bird-dog scouts to build relationships with players as young as nine or ten — a full tier below where even advisor work typically starts, and exactly the behavior a rule like this would be trying to catch. Enforcement, not the policy itself, is the hard part.

theScore's reporting backs this up from the inside. Agent Steve Bartlett described the current recruiting environment as "the wild, wild west," and pointed to prospects fielding pitches from multiple agencies as young as 12 or 13 — which, again, is advisor territory, not agent territory, whatever label gets used casually in the moment. Ray Petkau, who runs his own agency, was blunter about it: "What the heck are we doing? As an industry, we shouldn't be representing players at that young of an age. Preteens sometimes have agents." Petkau's complaint actually makes the case for precise terminology rather than against it — a preteen literally cannot have a commission agent under NCAA amateurism rules, so an industry that calls that relationship "having an agent" is misdescribing its own practice. That's not an outsider's criticism — that's a practicing agent saying the quiet part out loud, and part of the quiet part is that the label itself has gotten sloppy.

### Legitimate advisors are a different, more accountable category than bird dogs

None of this is an argument against advisors as a category — it's an argument against treating advisors and bird dogs as the same accountability problem, which they're not. A legitimate advisor discloses their fee up front, works from a track record a family can verify with other families and junior coaches, and has every incentive to protect a reputation that took years to build. A bird dog, by definition, operates with none of that — no disclosed terms, no verifiable history, often no more accountability than a phone number. The "wild west" framing that Bartlett and others use is real, and it's squarely aimed at the least accountable end of this market — the bird dogs and the agencies quietly using them — not at the advisors doing legitimate, necessary, disclosed work with NCAA-track families who have no other professional option available to them under the current amateurism rules.

### Poaching vs. prospecting: the line most agents and advisors draw for themselves

In the absence of a hard rule, most veteran agents and advisors have settled on an informal but fairly consistent standard: prospecting an unrepresented player is fair game, reaching out to a player who's already committed to another agent or advisor is not. It's not written down anywhere, and there's no enforcement mechanism beyond reputation — but reputation is the entire currency of this business, so it functions better than you'd expect. Those who cross that line tend to get known for it, and families and scouts factor that in.

## Winning the Family, Not Just the Player

### Why parents are often the real decision-makers

For most of a young player's prospect years — anywhere from age 12 to 15, when the relevant professional is almost always an advisor rather than a certified agent — the person actually deciding who represents their kid is a parent, not the player. That changes what "recruiting" actually looks like in practice. You're not selling a 13-year-old on your services; you're earning trust from an adult who's fielding calls from multiple advisors (and, if they're not careful about vetting, bird dogs) and trying to figure out who's telling the truth. The same dynamic carries forward once a player is closer to draft-eligible and a certified agent enters the picture — parents are still often heavily involved in that decision too, even as the player gains more say.

### What families are actually evaluating you on

The instinct for a new advisor or agent is to lead with upside — the NHL dream, the potential, the ceiling. Families who've done their homework are usually skeptical of exactly that pitch, and for good reason. Per [TSN's reporting](https://www.tsn.ca/chl/nhlpa-considering-restricting-agents-from-contacting-players-under-16-1.705352), former Flames GM Craig Button put the actual odds in blunt terms: "for all the kids born on the earth in one year, 40 of them are going to go on and play at least 400 games in the NHL." Any family that's talked to more than one advisor or agent has heard the big promises before, and the ones who overpromise are usually the ones who get quietly dropped a year later. What actually differentiates you is far less flashy: how often you communicate, whether you show up when nothing is happening (not just when there's a decision to make), and whether you talk about a long career path instead of just the next contract or the next commitment.

### Billet families and the trust network around a young player

Billet families are an underused source of both trust and information, and most agents and advisors don't invest in the relationship enough. A respectful, appropriate relationship with a player's billet family — one that stays inside team and SafeGuard/SafeSport boundaries rather than trying to work around them — means you often hear about a problem, an injury, or a development the moment it happens instead of weeks later. It also means the billet family becomes another voice vouching for you when the player's actual parents are trying to decide who to trust.

## The CHL-vs-NCAA Conversation Is Often Where Trust Is Won or Lost

Around age 15 or 16, most families hit the CHL-versus-NCAA decision point, and it tends to matter more for an advisor's relationship with that family than almost any other single conversation in the process — because this is squarely advisor territory, playing out before the player has any commission-agent relationship at all, and because the outcome determines whether a certified agent ever enters the picture in the traditional sense. We've covered the actual eligibility mechanics elsewhere — see [our guide to CHL and NCAA eligibility rules](/blog/chl-ncaa-eligibility-rules-2025) — but the relationship dynamic is worth separating out here.

This is a genuinely high-stakes decision for a family: one path pays for junior hockey and offers an accelerated pro track, the other preserves NCAA eligibility and a scholarship. Families are aware that an advisor's incentives aren't perfectly neutral here, and the ones who notice you giving an honest answer — including the parts that don't obviously benefit you — remember it. Laying out the real tradeoffs instead of steering toward whichever path is more convenient for your business is one of the fastest ways to earn a referral. It's also one of the fastest ways to lose a family's trust forever if they later feel like you nudged them for your own reasons.

## Differentiating Yourself in a Relationship-Driven Market

### Service beyond the contract: development, financial literacy, media training

Contract negotiation is table stakes for a certified agent; every certified agent can technically do it. What separates agencies in a crowded field — for their agents and their advisors alike — is what they offer beyond that. Ray Petkau's Alpha Hockey built NET360, a year-round program specifically for goalies, with a development director in constant season-long contact and an annual retreat. Petkau's own explanation, per [theScore's reporting](https://www.thescore.com/nhl/news/2377282), is a good way to think about specialization generally: "Some automotive shops will work on any makes that come in, and others may specialize in Mercedes Benz. So if you have a Mercedes Benz, you might go there instead."

### Building a niche (position-specific programs, a development facility, a specific league focus)

Some agencies have gone further and built physical infrastructure around their service model. Quartexx Management operates Hockey Etcetera, a Montreal facility giving clients access to a three-on-three rink, a gym, and recovery treatment — an investment COO Giordano Saputo acknowledges cuts into margins, which is exactly why most agencies don't attempt it. CAL Sports Management took a different structural approach, pairing a lawyer (co-founder Joe Caligiuri) with a 600-game NHL veteran (Matt Calvert) so contract expertise and on-ice development advice come from two specialists instead of one generalist — a model that serves both the NCAA-track families working with the agency's advisors and the draft-track clients working with its certified agents.

### Visibility and reputation — earned, not bought

Allan Walsh of Octagon Hockey built a different kind of differentiation — a public, consistent presence promoting his clients' achievements and speaking up on league issues, which has made him one of the more recognizable agents in the sport without ever needing to buy that visibility.

It's worth including the honest counter-narrative here too, because differentiation claims are easy to overstate. One 20-year veteran agent told theScore it's "virtually impossible to revolutionize the player representation business when its main revenue stream remains commission earned off NHL contracts" — and some of the league's top players have made the same point in a different way. Drew Doughty negotiated his own eight-year, $88-million extension without a certified agent in 2018, calling the commission he'd have paid "ridiculous," and Anthony Duclair tested free agency unrepresented in 2020, saying he "wanted to take ownership of my own life and my career." Neither example means service doesn't matter — for agents or advisors. It means the differentiation has to be real value a client couldn't easily replicate on their own — not a gimmick, and not a promise that outruns what you can actually deliver.

## The Long Game: Retention Is Part of Acquisition

### Your existing roster is your best recruiting tool

In a sport this small, most new clients don't come from a cold pitch at a showcase — they come from someone who's already watched you work. A current client's teammate, a billet family who liked how you handled a tough conversation, a junior coach who's seen you follow through over multiple seasons — that's where the referrals actually originate, for advisors building a junior roster just as much as for agents building a pro one. It's also why how you manage the clients you already have matters as much as how you find new ones; our [guide to managing a hockey agent's roster](/blog/how-hockey-agents-manage-their-roster) goes into what that looks like day to day.

### What makes a client refer their teammates and billet-family friends

Referrals happen when a client — or a client's family — feels like they were treated as a long-term relationship, not a transaction that ended once the ink dried. That holds true from the advisor relationship in junior hockey all the way through the grind of an [AHL or ECHL contract negotiation](/blog/ahl-echl-contract-negotiation-workflow), where an agent's relationship gets tested in ways a first junior conversation never does. The agents and advisors who do this well tend to keep some kind of running system — a spreadsheet, a notebook, or [a CRM built specifically for hockey agents and advisors](/blog/why-hockey-agents-need-a-crm) like Repline — so a teammate's contract year or a billet family's situation doesn't slip through the cracks between seasons. None of that replaces the relationship itself. It just means you're still showing up prepared three years in, which, in a market this small, is most of the job.

## FAQ

**What's the difference between a hockey agent and a hockey advisor?**
An agent is NHLPA-certified, works on commission, and represents players once they're off the NCAA-eligible track and into the draft or pro process, where NHLPA rules govern conduct and fees. An advisor is not NHLPA-certified — certification doesn't apply to the role — and works for a flat, pre-agreed fee with NCAA-track and junior players specifically because NCAA amateurism rules bar those players from having a commission agent. Both are legitimate professional categories; a bird dog, by contrast, is an uncertified, informal recruiter operating outside either framework.

**How young are hockey players when advisors or agents first contact them?**
Contact commonly starts around age 14, and per [theScore's reporting](https://www.thescore.com/nhl/news/2377141), some prospects field pitches from multiple advisors and agencies as young as 12 or 13. At that age, the relationship is almost always with an advisor rather than a certified agent, since NCAA-track and junior players can't be signed to a commission agent. Uncertified bird dogs have reportedly gone even younger, building relationships with players as young as nine or ten. The NHLPA has discussed, but not implemented, a rule that would bar certified agents from contacting players under 16.

**Is it legal or ethical for an agent or advisor to contact a player who's already represented?**
There's no blanket rule against it, but there's a strong informal industry norm against poaching an already-signed player. Most veterans draw a clear line between prospecting an unrepresented player and approaching someone who's already committed elsewhere, and agents or advisors who ignore that line tend to develop a reputation for it.

**Do hockey agents need to be NHLPA-certified to recruit players?**
Certified agents operate under the NHLPA's conduct and disclosure rules (see [our guide to NHLPA agent certification](/blog/nhlpa-agent-certification-guide)) once a player is draft- or pro-track. Advisors working with NCAA-track and junior players are not NHLPA-certified by design — certification doesn't apply to that role — and stay accountable instead through disclosed flat fees and a checkable track record. The real oversight gap is bird dogs: uncertified, informal recruiters who operate with no disclosed terms and no equivalent accountability at all.

**How do hockey agents and advisors get access to top junior and college prospects?**
Mostly through relationships with scouts, coaches, and GMs built over years, not through cold outreach. Those relationships are reciprocal — agents and advisors who share information and make themselves useful to scouts get more tips in return, and the ones who only show up looking for something tend to get cut off.

**What makes a family choose one agent or advisor over another?**
Usually not credentials or big promises — most families have already heard the NHL-dream pitch from several people by the time they talk to you. Communication cadence, honesty (including about odds and tradeoffs that don't favor you), and treating the relationship as long-term rather than transactional are what actually move families to commit.

**Can the same person represent both CHL and NCAA-track players?**
Not in the same capacity — a certified agent's commission relationship isn't available to NCAA-track players under amateurism rules, so that side of the business is handled by an advisor working on a flat fee, often at the same agency. Many agencies run both functions side by side, sometimes with the same person transitioning from advisor to agent for a client as that player turns pro. See [our guide to CHL and NCAA eligibility rules](/blog/chl-ncaa-eligibility-rules-2025) for the specifics of how the two paths differ.
