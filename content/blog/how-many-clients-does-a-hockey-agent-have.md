## Why Hockey Doesn't Have a Published Answer to This Question

Search "how many clients does an NFL agent have" and you'll get real numbers back — firm-by-firm breakdowns, agent counts, tiered rankings. Search the same question about baseball, and you'll find a widely cited benchmark tied to the 40-man roster. Search it about hockey, and you get almost nothing hockey-specific. No trade publication has ever run the hockey equivalent of the NFL agent census.

That's not because hockey lacks the data points — it's because nobody has assembled them into one place. This piece does that: real numbers where they exist (NHL contract databases, a couple of agency-specific reports, one recent PWHL survey), and an honest framework where they don't. We're not going to manufacture a tidy "the answer is 22 clients" statistic, because that number doesn't exist in any usable form. What follows is what the data actually supports.

## What Other Sports Tell Us About Agent Roster Sizes

### NFL: From 3-Client Boutiques to 146-Client Powerhouses

The most detailed public breakdown of agent roster size comes from football. [National Football Post's "Agents, by the numbers"](https://www.nationalfootballpost.com/2008-2018-nfp-archive/the-agents-journal/agents-by-the-numbers/) tallied client counts across every certified NFL agency and found a massive spread. Rosenhaus Sports topped the list with 146 clients spread across just 2 agents. CAA Football had 104 clients, also with 2 agents. Athletes First carried 91 clients across 5 agents, Priority Sports had 84 across 5, and SportsStars represented 78 clients with 7 agents on staff.

Below the giants, the market sorts into clear bands: 6 firms had 40–49 clients, 10 firms had 30–39, 9 firms had 20–29, 31 firms had 10–19, and a long tail of 100 firms had just 3–9 clients each.

The more telling number is how those clients are distributed by value. The nine largest agencies represented roughly 671 players combined. Fifty-five mid-size firms accounted for about 900 more. The 100 smallest firms held around 430 players — many of them, as the report put it, "mostly sitting at the bottom of a roster or on the practice squad." Raw client count on its own tells you almost nothing about revenue, influence, or service quality. A 146-client agency and a 6-client agency can both be functioning exactly as designed.

### Baseball: About 3 Clients Per Agency Per 40-Man Roster

Baseball's benchmark is narrower and more commonly cited: across the roughly 400-plus MLBPA-registered agents and agencies, the average works out to about 3 clients per agency on a given 40-man roster. That's a tight number by comparison to football's spread — and it makes outliers like Scott Boras stand out even more. Boras is the sport's best-known mega-agent, though even his exact client count isn't consistent across sources — some coverage puts it around 76, other reporting cites 113. That gap isn't a data error so much as a reminder that even in a well-covered sport with a strong players' union, published client counts vary by source, methodology, and snapshot date. Treat any single-number claim — including some in this article — as directional rather than exact.

### The Three-Tier Pattern That Shows Up Everywhere

Across sports, agencies tend to cluster into the same three tiers. [Kevin Tarca's breakdown of sports agency business models](https://www.kevintarca.com/post/sports-agency-business-model) frames it as boutique (fewer than 10 clients), mid-size (10–25), and mega-agency (40-plus). What separates the tiers isn't ambition — it's a capacity question every agent eventually asks, which Tarca sums up directly: the real constraint is "how many clients can I have while still making sure I service each guy properly."

Revenue scale explains why mega-agencies can carry high headcounts without any single agent being overloaded. By one snapshot, CAA's football book alone represented $10.9 billion in contracts, Wasserman $4.2 billion, and Excel Sports $3.6 billion. Those totals are split across dozens of agents inside each firm. A 100-client agency isn't one person managing 100 relationships — it's 10 or 15 agents each managing a boutique-sized book under one shared brand.

## What We Actually Know About Hockey Agent Rosters

### NHL Client Concentration Is Extreme

Hockey doesn't have a recurring, publicly maintained agent census the way football does, but one detailed snapshot exists. [Sports Agent Blog's NHL Agent Leaderboard](https://sportsagentblog.com/2020/11/02/nhl-agent-leaderboard/) found Craig Oster of Newport Sports leading all NHL agents with 76 individual client contracts — at the opposite extreme, agents like Stephen Screnci represent a single elite client (Alex Ovechkin) and still rank inside the top 40 by total contract value. The same analysis found the top seven agencies controlling 46% of all NHL contracts, along with 54% of total contract value and 52% of aggregate cap hit.

Worth flagging clearly: this data is from 2020. Rosters, trades, and free agency turn over every year, so treat these as directional evidence of concentration in the market, not a current-year leaderboard. But the underlying pattern — a small number of firms controlling a large share of contracts, with individual agent counts ranging from 1 to over 70 — is exactly the same shape the NFL data showed.

### Newport Sports and the Mega-Agency Model

Newport Sports Management, branded publicly as "The Hockey Agency," is hockey's largest agency by a wide margin. Founded in 1981, it now represents more than 330 total clients across all levels of the sport, with figures on active NHL clients ranging from roughly 125 to 150-plus depending on the source and snapshot date — [PuckPedia](https://puckpedia.com/agency/Newport%20Sports%20Management%20Inc.) and [hockeyagencies.com](https://www.hockeyagencies.com/hockey-agency/newport-sports-management/) both track Newport as the largest public NHL agency book by active contract value.

The lesson is the same one the NFL numbers taught: that 330-plus client total isn't one agent's workload. It's split across dozens of agents inside the firm, each carrying a smaller, more manageable book. A single Newport agent's actual client list looks nothing like the firm-wide total — which is exactly why comparing a solo practitioner's roster to a mega-agency's published headcount is comparing two different things.

### The PWHL Is Building Its Own Agent Market From Scratch

The women's professional game offers a rare look at an agent market forming in real time. [A Yahoo Sports report](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/womens-hockey-agencies-continue-grow-142437415.html) found 67 agents currently certified on the PWHLPA's list, though not all of them actively represent players in the league today. A "Big Six" group of agencies — Pro Vision Sports Agency, 3G Sports, Cook Stark Management, Lora Athletes, CAA, and Quartexx — leads the market, with most of them carrying more than 20 players on their rosters. Cook Stark alone had more players drafted than any other agency at the 2026 PWHL Draft, placing 15 players in a single draft class.

With the league now at 12 teams, this market is still young and still consolidating. It's worth watching over the next few draft cycles rather than treating today's numbers as settled — the PWHL agent landscape a few years from now may look nothing like the "Big Six" snapshot does today.

## Why Hockey Client Capacity Doesn't Translate From Other Sports

### A Huge Share of "Clients" Are Minors on the Advisor Track

Here's the structural difference that breaks any direct comparison to football or baseball: a large share of the people hockey agents work with aren't legally clients of an agent at all. [As TimTurkHockey explains](https://www.timturkhockey.com/hockey-agents-vs-family-advisors/), NCAA rules bar any still-eligible player from signing with an agent, full stop. Anyone advising a player who might play NCAA hockey has to operate as a fixed-fee "family advisor" instead — paid a flat, pre-agreed rate rather than a commission on a contract that doesn't exist yet.

That means a 16-year-old AAA prospect and a 24-year-old NHL veteran can both get casually lumped into someone's "client list," but they need completely different service models — one is a flat-fee advisory relationship built around development and recruiting, the other is a commission-based negotiation relationship built around contract terms. Counting them the same way, the way NFL or MLB rosters can be counted, hides more than it reveals. Our [CHL/NCAA eligibility guide](/blog/chl-ncaa-eligibility-rules-2025) breaks down exactly where those lines sit by league and age.

### One Client, Multiple Leagues, Multiple Countries

An NFL client plays in one league under one set of rules for the length of their career. A hockey client might move through the CHL (Canadian Hockey League, the top junior circuit), the USHL, NCAA, AHL (the primary pro development league one step below the NHL), ECHL (a lower-tier pro league), the KHL, and multiple European leagues — all within a five-year span. Each stop has its own eligibility rules, contract structures, and governing bodies.

That mobility is the single clearest reason a hockey capacity number can't be borrowed from another sport. An NFL or MLB agent managing 15 clients is managing 15 versions of essentially one compliance framework. A hockey agent managing 15 clients across junior, college, AHL, and European rosters is managing four or five different rulebooks simultaneously, often for the same client within a single season.

### Hockey's Compliance Calendar Hits Every Client at Once

Hockey also compresses its highest-stakes deadlines into short, predictable windows that hit every eligible client at the same time — not spread across the calendar. Club-elected salary arbitration notifications are due by mid-June, with player-elected filings typically due in the first week of July. Free agency for unrestricted free agents opens July 1. Offer sheets for restricted free agents can only be submitted between July 1 and December 1, and once one is signed, [the player's original team has just seven days to match it or lose the player](https://soundofhockey.com/2025/03/20/explaining-how-offer-sheets-work-in-the-nhl/) for draft-pick compensation. December 1 also functions as a hard deadline for signing that season's RFAs. Then the trade deadline lands in late February or early March, triggering another round of contract and no-move clause conversations. [The current CBA](https://www.espn.com/nhl/story/_/id/45732725/nhl-nhlpa-cba-new-rules-salary-cap-trades-ebugs-84-games) adjusted several of the underlying rules but kept this same basic shape.

The practical effect: these dates don't spread evenly across an agent's roster. If you represent 10 RFAs, all 10 of their qualifying-offer and offer-sheet windows land in the same six-week stretch. A 20-client roster doesn't distribute deadlines — it stacks them into collision weeks. Our [draft day prep checklist](/blog/draft-day-prep-checklist-hockey-advisors) and [AHL/ECHL contract negotiation workflow guide](/blog/ahl-echl-contract-negotiation-workflow) go deeper on managing those specific crunch points.

## A Framework for Estimating Your Own Realistic Capacity

Since no hockey-specific benchmark exists, the more useful exercise is reasoning through your own numbers rather than borrowing someone else's.

**Weight your roster by client mix, not headcount.** Five junior clients who need weekly contact with families, regular scouting updates, and NCAA/CHL eligibility monitoring can be a heavier workload than 15 professional clients who mostly need quarterly check-ins and deadline tracking. Two agents with "12 clients" can be doing wildly different amounts of work.

**Model your capacity across the season, not just in a slow month.** A roster that feels manageable in October can become unmanageable in the July 1–December 1 window, when free agency, arbitration, and offer sheets all land close together. Size your roster for your worst six weeks, not your best six weeks.

**Account for whether you're solo or team-supported.** The high headcounts you see at Newport or the NFL mega-agencies only work because they're split across many agents. A solo practitioner's realistic ceiling looks nothing like a firm-wide total.

**Factor in per-client admin surface area.** A client playing in one league in one country takes less coordination than one moving between the CHL, NCAA, and Europe in the same year. Multi-league, multi-country clients eat disproportionately more time per client than the raw headcount suggests.

And regardless of how you track any of this — a spreadsheet, a whiteboard, or a purpose-built CRM like Repline — the underlying constraint is the same: every additional client adds fixed deadline-tracking overhead that doesn't shrink just because the season gets busier.

## Signs Your Roster Has Outgrown Your Capacity

A few practical warning signs tend to show up before a roster becomes an actual problem:

- You've missed, or come close to missing, an arbitration filing or offer-sheet response deadline.
- Families are telling you — directly or indirectly — that they can't reach you during the season.
- Junior clients aren't getting regular video or scouting feedback anymore; contact has become reactive instead of scheduled.
- On July 1 or draft day, you're triaging which calls happen first instead of having already planned the sequence.
- You're relying on memory, not a system, to know who's eligible for what and when — which contracts are up, which RFAs need qualifying offers, which junior clients have an NCAA commitment deadline approaching.

None of these on their own means you're over capacity. All of them together, more than once a year, usually does.

## FAQ

**How many players does the average NHL agent represent?**
There's no single reliable average, because the range is too extreme to summarize with one number. A 2020 snapshot found agents representing anywhere from a single elite client to 76 individual contracts, with the top seven agencies controlling 46% of all NHL contracts. Any "average" would blend mega-agency totals — which are really many agents' books combined — with true solo counts, producing a number that doesn't describe anyone's actual day-to-day roster.

**What's the difference between a hockey agent and a family advisor?**
An agent works on commission and can only represent players who are no longer NCAA-eligible. A family advisor works for a fixed, pre-agreed fee and is the only legal option for a player who might still play NCAA hockey, since [NCAA rules bar eligible players from signing with agents](https://www.timturkhockey.com/hockey-agents-vs-family-advisors/) at all.

**How many clients does Newport Sports, hockey's biggest agency, have?**
More than 330 total clients across all levels, with active NHL clients estimated between roughly 125 and 150-plus depending on the source. That total is split across dozens of agents inside the firm — not the workload of any single person.

**Is there a legal or NHLPA limit on how many clients an agent can represent?**
No. NHLPA certification governs conduct, fee disclosure, and commission caps — not headcount. There's nothing stopping a certified agent from taking on an unlimited number of clients; the limiting factor is entirely practical capacity, not regulation. Our [NHLPA agent certification guide](/blog/nhlpa-agent-certification-guide) covers what certification actually requires.

**Why do junior hockey clients take up more time than pro clients?**
Junior and NCAA-track clients are usually on the family-advisor side of the business, which means more frequent family contact, ongoing NCAA/CHL eligibility monitoring, and regular scouting and development conversations — a fundamentally more hands-on relationship than a pro client who mainly needs contract and deadline management.

**How is the PWHL agent market different from the NHL's?**
It's newer, smaller, and far less concentrated. With 67 certified PWHLPA agents and a "Big Six" group of agencies most carrying 20-plus players, the market is still forming rather than settled — a meaningfully different picture from the NHL's decades-old, heavily concentrated agent landscape.
